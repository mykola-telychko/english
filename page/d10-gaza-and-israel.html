Here's a dialogue between two friends — Michael and Alex:

Michael: Hey, have you been following the news about Gaza lately? It's pretty intense.

Alex: Yeah, I have. It's a really complicated situation. What do you think about it?

Michael: Honestly, I feel terrible for the civilians. So many innocent people are suffering, especially children.

Alex: I agree, it's heartbreaking. But you have to understand that Israel is responding to Hamas attacks. They can't just do nothing after what happened.

Michael: That's true, but the scale of the response is what worries me. So many civilian casualties... at what point does it become too much?

Alex: I get your point. But Hamas hides among civilians, uses hospitals and schools. That makes it extremely difficult for Israel to fight back without civilian losses.

Michael: Sure, but Palestinians have been living under blockade for years. No freedom of movement, limited food and medicine. Don't you think that creates the conditions for extremism to grow?

Alex: That's a fair point actually. The situation in Gaza was already terrible before all this. But does that justify attacking civilians in Israel?

Michael: No, absolutely not. Killing innocent people is never justified, on either side. I just think we need to understand the root causes.

Alex: I agree with that. The core issue is that both sides believe they have a right to the same land. This conflict goes back decades.

Michael: Right. There were several peace attempts — Oslo, Camp David — but they all failed in the end.

Alex: Both sides made mistakes in those negotiations. It's hard to say who was more at fault.

Michael: Exactly. And now ordinary people on both sides are paying the price. Israelis live in fear of rockets, Palestinians live under constant military pressure.

Alex: So what do you think the solution is?

Michael: Honestly? I think the only real solution is two separate states — Israel and Palestine living side by side. But that seems so far away right now.

Alex: Yeah, with so much hatred and distrust on both sides, it's hard to imagine peace anytime soon. But I hope someday it happens.

Michael: Me too. At the end of the day, regular people just want to live in peace, raise their kids, and have a normal life.

Alex: Couldn't agree more. It's the politicians and extremists who keep this going, not ordinary people.

Vocabulary to note:

  • blockade — блокада
  • civilian casualties — жертви серед мирного населення
  • root causes — першопричини
  • extremism — екстремізм
  • negotiations — переговори
  • distrust — недовіра

Here's the continuation of the dialogue:

Michael: You know what's also interesting? How differently countries react to this conflict.

Alex: Oh yeah, totally. Like the US always sides with Israel — sending weapons, blocking UN resolutions...

Michael: Well, Israel is their closest ally in the Middle East. It's not just about values, it's about geopolitics and strategic interests.

Alex: Sure, but it looks really bad when the US vetoes ceasefire resolutions at the UN while thousands of civilians are dying.

Michael: I agree it's controversial. On the other hand, European countries are more split. Some support Israel's right to defend itself, others are pushing hard for a ceasefire.

Alex: Yeah, like Ireland and Spain were pretty vocal about Palestinian rights. While Germany and the UK leaned more toward Israel, at least at the beginning.

Michael: And then you have the Arab world — countries like Jordan, Egypt, they express solidarity with Palestinians but don't really open their borders or take refugees in.

Alex: That's actually a really uncomfortable truth. Arab countries use the Palestinian cause politically but don't do much practically to help.

Michael: Exactly. Kuwait, Saudi Arabia — they have enormous wealth but Palestinian refugees live in terrible conditions in Lebanon and Jordan for generations.

Alex: And Russia and China? They condemn Israel but it's clearly just to oppose the West, not because they genuinely care about Palestinians.

Michael: Right. It's all a geopolitical game. Nobody's hands are completely clean here.

Alex: So basically everyone is using this conflict for their own interests?

Michael: Pretty much. Which makes finding a real solution even harder.

Alex: Let's talk about propaganda then, because I think that's a huge part of why this conflict is so hard to understand.

Michael: Absolutely. Both sides have very powerful propaganda machines.

Alex: Israel is really good at framing everything as self-defense. Every operation has a name that sounds defensive — "Operation Protective Edge", "Iron Swords"...

Michael: And they're very good at working with Western media. Israeli spokespeople speak perfect English, they're media trained, they know how to communicate their message effectively.

Alex: Meanwhile Palestinian voices are often less heard in mainstream Western media. There's a real imbalance there.

Michael: But Hamas also uses propaganda heavily. They film themselves among civilians, they exaggerate casualty numbers sometimes, they present every Palestinian as a pure victim with no mention of rocket attacks on Israeli cities.

Alex: That's true. And social media has made everything worse. You scroll through Instagram or TikTok and you see either completely pro-Israel or completely pro-Palestinian content. No nuance whatsoever.

Michael: The algorithm just feeds you what you already believe. So people end up in these echo chambers where they only see one side of the story.

Alex: And then they go online and attack anyone who disagrees with them. I've seen friendships destroyed over this conflict.

Michael: Same. I had a colleague who stopped talking to me because I said I understood why Israel responded militarily. She thought that meant I supported killing children.

Alex: And on the other side, people who express sympathy for Palestinians get called antisemitic immediately. It shuts down any real conversation.

Michael: That's the propaganda working perfectly. Label your opponent, discredit them, end the debate.

Alex: Both sides do it. "You support Hamas." "You're a genocide supporter." Nobody actually listens anymore.

Michael: And the media plays into this too. Western outlets like CNN or BBC are seen as pro-Israel by pro-Palestinians. Al Jazeera is seen as pro-Palestinian by pro-Israelis.

Alex: So where do you even get objective information?

Michael: Honestly, it's really hard. You have to read multiple sources, including ones you disagree with. Look at on-the-ground reporting, human rights organizations like Amnesty International or B'Tselem.

Alex: Even those are controversial though. Some people say Amnesty is biased against Israel.

Michael: True. I think the honest answer is that there is no perfectly neutral source. You just have to be aware of each source's perspective and read critically.

Alex: What worries me most is the effect on younger generations. Kids on TikTok are getting very one-sided views of this conflict.

Michael: Yeah, and it's affecting how they view Jewish people or Arab people in general. There's been a massive spike in antisemitism and Islamophobia around the world since the conflict escalated.

Alex: That's really dangerous. People start blaming entire groups for the actions of governments or armed groups.

Michael: A Jewish student in Paris has nothing to do with Israeli military policy. And a Muslim student in London has nothing to do with Hamas.

Alex: But people treat them like they do. That's the real-world impact of propaganda — it dehumanizes entire groups.

Michael: And once you dehumanize people, it becomes easier to justify violence against them. That's how hatred spreads.

Alex: So what can ordinary people like us do?

Michael: I think the most important thing is to stay curious and humble. Admit that this is complicated. Don't pretend you have all the answers.

Alex: And actually listen to people from both sides. Not just politicians or activists, but regular Israelis and regular Palestinians.

Michael: Exactly. Because at the end of the day, they're the ones living with the consequences of this conflict every single day.

Alex: I really hope my kids grow up in a world where this is finally resolved.

Michael: Me too, man. Me too.

Alex: You know, at the end of the day, it's all about finding common ground. Like, both sides need to sit down and talk, actually.

Michael: Totally. I mean, without dialogue, nothing changes. You know what I mean?

Alex: Yeah, exactly. And like, the international community should step in more, you know? Not just talk, but actually do something.

Michael: I agree. At the end of the day, it's the leaders who have to compromise. Like, no more excuses.

Alex: Right. And you know, ordinary people suffer the most. I mean, it's heartbreaking.

Michael: Totally. Like, imagine living in fear every day. Actually, that's no way to live.

Alex: Yeah. At the end of the day, we all want peace. You know?

Michael: For sure. Let's hope it happens soon, man.

Alex: Honestly, I think the hardest part is that people often talk past each other.

Michael: Yeah, that’s exactly it. One side focuses on security, the other on justice, and before you know it, nobody really listens.

Alex: And that’s the thing, right? People tend to react emotionally, and then the whole conversation gets lost.

Michael: Sure, but that doesn’t mean we should give up. We’ve got to keep talking, even when it feels frustrating.

Alex: I totally agree. At the end of the day, you can’t solve a problem like this by staying silent.

Michael: Exactly. It’s not about pretending everything is fine. It’s about being honest and keeping the conversation going.

Alex: And maybe that’s the first step — just trying to understand where the other person is coming from.

Michael: That’s true. Even if we don’t agree on everything, we can still show respect and listen properly.

Alex: I hope so. Because otherwise it just turns into the same old cycle of blame and anger.

Michael: Yeah, and that’s the last thing anyone needs. We need less shouting and more real dialogue.

Alex: No doubt about that. And honestly, that’s probably what peace really starts with.

Michael: For sure. One step at a time, one conversation at a time.

Alex: Now that we are talking about practical steps, what I mean is that ordinary people can also support humanitarian work without pretending that one donation will solve the whole conflict. From my point of view, the most responsible approach would be to support reliable organizations that provide food, medical care, clean water, shelter, and psychological support to civilians. If people decide to help, they should check how an organization works, where the money goes, and whether its information is independently confirmed.

Michael: I agree, and what I'd suggest is that people should be careful about sharing emotional posts before checking the source. At the end of the day, a shocking photograph or a dramatic claim can spread around the world within minutes, even when the date, location, or context is unclear. What I mean is that compassion and critical thinking should go together: we can care deeply about suffering while still asking whether the information is accurate. If we share something false, we may unintentionally increase fear and hostility.

Alex: That's a fair point, and I think it is especially important when the subject is as sensitive as this one. From my point of view, a useful question is, "What do we actually know, and what are we only assuming?" If a report comes from an unknown account, contains extreme language, or gives no date or evidence, I would wait before treating it as fact. What I'd suggest is comparing several sources, including sources that do not share the same political position, and paying attention to what they agree on as well as where they disagree.

Michael: Exactly. What I mean is that responsible discussion does not require us to have complete certainty about every detail, but it does require us to be honest about the limits of our knowledge. If we are not sure whether a claim is true, we can say, "I have seen this report, but I cannot confirm it yet." At the end of the day, admitting uncertainty is not weakness; it is a way of protecting the conversation from exaggeration. What I'd suggest is using careful language instead of repeating accusations as though they had already been legally established.

Alex: I couldn't agree more, although I know that some people think careful language means avoiding the truth. What I mean is that we can condemn attacks on civilians, forced displacement, hostage-taking, and the destruction of essential services clearly, while still distinguishing between verified facts, allegations, and personal opinions. From my point of view, precision makes moral criticism stronger rather than weaker. If we use the same standards for every side, people may be more willing to listen, even when they strongly disagree with our conclusions.

Michael: That's an important principle, and what I'd suggest is that we should apply it consistently, especially when discussing responsibility. At the end of the day, criticizing a government, a military decision, or an armed group is not the same as blaming an entire ethnic or religious community. What I mean is that Jewish people around the world should not be held responsible for every decision made by the Israeli government, just as Muslim or Arab people should not be held responsible for the actions of Hamas or any other armed organization. If we fail to make that distinction, political disagreement can quickly become discrimination.

Alex: Yes, and from my point of view, that distinction should be repeated whenever the conversation becomes emotional. If someone says, "They are all responsible," I would ask which specific institution, leader, or decision they are referring to. What I mean is that collective blame may sound simple, but it usually hides the differences between civilians, political leaders, soldiers, activists, and armed groups. What I'd suggest is bringing the discussion back to individual rights and responsibilities, because every person deserves to be judged by their own actions rather than by their identity.

Michael: That makes sense, and it also explains why education matters so much. At the end of the day, young people need more than headlines and short videos; they need the ability to recognize historical context, question sources, and understand how propaganda works. What I'd suggest is teaching them to ask who created a message, what the message wants them to feel, and what information may have been left out. If they learn those habits early, they may become less vulnerable to hate speech and less likely to repeat claims that dehumanize other people.

Alex: I agree, and I think schools and families should create spaces where difficult questions can be discussed without immediately labeling someone as a bad person. What I mean is that a student may repeat a problematic idea because they have seen it online, not because they fully understand or support it. From my point of view, the best response would be to ask questions, explain why the idea is harmful, and provide more reliable information. If we only respond with anger, the person may become defensive and look for an even more extreme community.

Michael: Exactly, although there must also be clear limits when speech encourages violence or attacks people because of their identity. What I'd suggest is combining patience with responsibility: listen to genuine questions, challenge inaccurate claims, and refuse to normalize threats or hateful language. At the end of the day, freedom of discussion cannot mean that vulnerable people are expected to accept abuse without protection. What I mean is that respectful dialogue is open to disagreement, but it is not neutral about the dignity and safety of civilians.

Alex: That's a distinction worth remembering. From my point of view, a future peace process would also need to address both immediate security concerns and the deeper political conditions that keep the conflict going. If negotiations focus only on stopping the violence for a short period, the same problems may return when the pressure becomes weaker. What I mean is that a lasting agreement would probably require security guarantees, the release of hostages and prisoners through lawful arrangements, humanitarian access, political representation, and a realistic plan for rebuilding trust over many years.

Michael: I see what you mean, and what I'd suggest is that we should be realistic about how difficult such a process would be without becoming convinced that it is impossible. At the end of the day, peace agreements are usually built through many limited steps rather than one dramatic conversation that solves everything. If leaders can reduce violence, protect civilians, allow aid to reach people, and create channels for direct communication, they may gradually make larger political negotiations possible. What I mean is that small improvements should not be dismissed simply because they do not immediately produce a final settlement.

Alex: That's true, but I also think small steps need to be connected to a clear long-term goal, otherwise they may become temporary pauses without meaningful change. From my point of view, people need to know what the process is trying to achieve and how progress will be measured. What I'd suggest is setting specific commitments, independent monitoring, and regular public updates so that both sides can see whether promises are being kept. If there is no accountability, distrust will probably return as soon as the first serious difficulty appears.

Michael: I agree, and what I mean is that accountability should apply to everyone involved, including political leaders and powerful international actors. If one side is expected to follow rules while another side is excused because it is an ally, the process will not seem fair and ordinary people will lose faith in it. At the end of the day, international law and human rights should not depend on which country or group a person supports. What I'd suggest is judging actions by the same basic standards: protection of civilians, respect for human dignity, and a genuine effort to resolve disputes without unnecessary violence.

Alex: I couldn't agree more. I know we may still disagree about history, responsibility, and which political solution is most realistic, but from my point of view, we can agree on some basic principles. Civilians should be protected, humanitarian aid should reach those who need it, antisemitism and Islamophobia should be rejected, and people should not be treated as enemies simply because of their identity. If we start with those principles, perhaps we can have a more honest conversation about the difficult questions that remain.

Michael: Exactly, and that's probably the most useful conclusion we can reach tonight. What I mean is that understanding a conflict does not require us to choose between empathy and evidence, or between security and justice; we need to take all of these concerns seriously while refusing to justify harm to civilians. What I'd suggest is staying informed, checking information before sharing it, supporting humanitarian efforts, and speaking to other people with enough humility to recognize that our own perspective may be incomplete. At the end of the day, peace may begin with governments and institutions, but it also depends on whether ordinary people are willing to protect truth, dignity, and dialogue.

Alex: Before we finish, I would like to talk about what happens after the immediate violence decreases, because from my point of view, stopping the fighting is necessary but it is only the beginning of a much longer process. What I mean is that people who have lost homes, relatives, schools, hospitals, and their sense of safety will need practical support as well as political promises. If reconstruction is not organized carefully and fairly, frustration may grow again, so the international community would have to combine financial assistance with transparent planning and local participation.

Michael: I agree, and what I'd suggest is that recovery should be measured not only by the number of buildings repaired but also by the quality of everyday life. At the end of the day, people need access to education, healthcare, employment, clean water, and a safe place to live before they can begin to feel that the future is possible. What I mean is that rebuilding trust requires more than repairing physical infrastructure; it requires showing people that their dignity and needs are being taken seriously. If communities are included in decisions about reconstruction, the results are more likely to be useful and accepted.

Alex: That's a very important point, and I think the same principle applies to education for children who have experienced fear and displacement. From my point of view, schools should provide a stable environment where students can learn, ask questions, and gradually recover a sense of normality. What I'd suggest is combining academic support with access to trained counselors, because children may carry stress that is not immediately visible to adults. If education is ignored, an entire generation may face difficulties that continue long after the political situation has changed.

Michael: Exactly. What I mean is that psychological support should not be treated as an optional extra, especially when people have lived through repeated loss and uncertainty. If families can receive reliable medical and social services, they may have a better chance of rebuilding their lives and preventing trauma from being passed on through fear and anger. At the end of the day, a sustainable peace process has to care about both public institutions and individual experiences. What I'd suggest is listening to local doctors, teachers, community leaders, and families instead of designing every solution from a distant office.

Alex: I couldn't agree more, although I realize that local participation can be complicated when communities are divided and many people no longer trust institutions. What I mean is that different groups may have completely different ideas about who should be responsible for security, public services, and political representation. From my point of view, the process would need independent observers and clear rules so that no group could control every decision without accountability. If people see that the process is fair, they may gradually become more willing to cooperate even when they still disagree.

Michael: That's true, and what I'd suggest is creating several channels for communication rather than expecting one official negotiation to solve every problem. At the end of the day, political leaders, local authorities, civil society organizations, religious communities, and ordinary residents may all have different concerns that need to be addressed. What I mean is that a formal agreement can provide a framework, but everyday cooperation is what makes that framework real. If people can work together on practical issues such as water, healthcare, schools, and transport, they may begin to rebuild trust from the ground up.

Alex: I see what you mean, and I think practical cooperation can sometimes be more persuasive than speeches about reconciliation. If two communities manage to solve a shared problem together, they may start to see one another as partners in a limited but meaningful way. From my point of view, that does not erase historical pain or political disagreement, but it creates evidence that cooperation is possible. What I'd suggest is supporting projects where people from different backgrounds have a clear common goal and can see the results of their work directly.

Michael: Yes, and it is important not to demand forgiveness or friendship before people feel safe enough to consider those possibilities. What I mean is that reconciliation cannot be forced through slogans, because people need time to mourn, remember, and express anger without being told to move on immediately. At the end of the day, a genuine peace process should make room for truth, justice, and accountability as well as reconciliation. If victims feel that their experiences have been ignored, an agreement may look stable on paper while remaining deeply fragile in reality.

Alex: That's a difficult but necessary point. From my point of view, justice does not always mean that every person will agree about the past, but it should mean that serious violations are investigated and that victims are treated with respect. What I'd suggest is supporting independent investigations and giving people access to reliable information about what happened, even when the findings are uncomfortable for powerful groups. If accountability is avoided completely, resentment may remain beneath the surface and make future cooperation much harder.

Michael: I agree, although I also think justice must be pursued carefully so that it does not become another excuse for collective punishment. What I mean is that responsibility should be based on evidence and individual actions, not on nationality, religion, or political identity. At the end of the day, a fair process has to protect the rights of victims while also respecting basic legal standards for anyone accused of wrongdoing. What I'd suggest is separating the need for accountability from the desire for revenge, because revenge may satisfy anger for a moment but rarely creates lasting security.

Alex: That's exactly right, and I think this is where language can either calm a situation or make it worse. If public figures use words that describe an entire population as less human or permanently dangerous, ordinary people may feel that violence against them is acceptable. From my point of view, leaders should choose words that recognize suffering without encouraging hatred, and journalists should explain context rather than simply repeating the most extreme statements. What I mean is that responsible language cannot create peace on its own, but irresponsible language can make peace much more difficult.

Michael: Absolutely, and what I'd suggest is that every person who speaks publicly should ask what effect their words might have on people who are already frightened or angry. At the end of the day, a message may be legally permitted and still be deeply irresponsible if it encourages discrimination or violence. If leaders, media organizations, and online users show more care with their language, they may help reduce the pressure that pushes communities further apart. What I mean is that accuracy, empathy, and accountability should remain connected, especially during a crisis.

Alex: I couldn't agree more. From my point of view, the best we can do as individuals is to remain informed without becoming addicted to outrage, support civilians without turning suffering into a competition, and challenge hatred regardless of who expresses it. What I'd suggest is asking ourselves three questions before we share a claim or join an argument: Is it verified, is it fair, and could it harm someone unnecessarily? If we keep asking those questions, we may not solve the conflict, but we can avoid making the public conversation even more hostile.

Michael: That's a thoughtful way to end, and I think it summarizes the responsibility ordinary people actually have. What I mean is that we cannot personally negotiate a peace agreement, rebuild every school, or protect every civilian, but we can decide what information we spread and how we speak about people we may never meet. At the end of the day, lasting peace will require difficult decisions from leaders and institutions, but it will also require millions of smaller decisions based on dignity, patience, and honesty. What I'd suggest is taking one step at a time, staying open to learning, and refusing to let fear decide how we see other human beings.
